The Shared Decision Room

Extended Reality, or XR, means using headsets or a large screen room to see and walk around a 3D model instead of reading flat drawings. This piece shows how XR can help many groups make big decisions on Canada’s warship programs faster, with a clear record of who decided what. Canada is rebuilding its navy

The largest naval build-up since the Second World War, and the largest defence purchase in Canadian history alongside it.

River-class destroyers

$84.5B

to buy up to 15 ships at Irving Shipbuilding in Halifax, and about $306 billion over the life of the fleet, which covers decades of operating and maintaining them (Parliamentary Budget Officer, 2022).

New submarines

Up to 12

On July 6, 2026, Canada chose the German company TKMS as its preferred builder. Preferred means chosen to negotiate with: it is not a signed deal, and the submarines are not confirmed to be built in Canada.

Why it matters

Protecting the Arctic and meeting Canada’s commitments to NATO and NORAD, the North American defence partnership. Canada’s four current Victoria-class submarines are aging and largely out of service; the new fleet would replace them.

Two yards, and a growing cluster

Canada’s National Shipbuilding Strategy runs on two big yards: Irving in Halifax builds the combat ships, and Seaspan in Vancouver builds the non-combat ships and is lined up for in-Canada support of the new submarines.

Alongside them, Kraken Robotics, a Canadian sonar and subsea-technology company (sonar uses sound to picture the sea floor), is expanding into Nova Scotia with a new Dartmouth battery plant opened in early 2026. It does not build ships and is not on these programs, but it shows the region is already building real naval technology, and it is the kind of regional partner a growing Nova Scotia marine-technology cluster could draw on.

The point of this piece: programs this large succeed or fail on big, shared decisions. The pages that follow show where those decisions go wrong today, what XR can honestly fix, and one standout idea: the shared decision room.

The problem is not too few workers. It is redoing work, and too many separate views.

The big shortage figures of 200,000 to 250,000 workers describe the whole United States industry, not Irving. Irving’s own stated need is about 400 to 600 more tradespeople: a manageable scale-up, not a crisis.

Be honest about what works: three XR uses, ranked by proof

XR is often sold with impressive percentages. Most do not survive contact with the evidence.

1 · VR training for welders

PROVEN

VR means a headset that puts you in a fully simulated practice space. A controlled study found VR-assisted welding training beat traditional training across four weld types while saving about $244 per student in materials and time (Stone, Watts and Zhong, 2011). That US study is the signal; the Canadian anchor is Red Seal welding training, and results are still to be confirmed at Canadian warship grade.

2 · AR instructions on the shop floor

PROMISING, UNPROVEN

AR means digital guidance laid over the real work in front of you. Picture a pipefitter in the Halifax yard fitting a complex pipe run in a tight compartment: instead of walking back to a paper drawing, the next connection is shown on the actual pipe in front of them. The idea is sound. It just has not been measured at a yard like Irving yet, and a plain tablet may do much of the same job for less.

3 · The immersive walk-through

MOSTLY FOR SHOW

Impressive to look at, but for everyday coordination an ordinary screen shows the same information for far less money. One valuable exception: high-stakes decisions where several groups must agree in the same 3D space. That exception is view 4.

Ground rule used throughout: vendor and marketing numbers are not treated as evidence. Only measured comparisons against the normal way of working count.

The standout idea: one ship, one room, every decision on the record

Everyone walks through the same 3D ship and sees the current design. Every important part carries a simple record: who approved it, when, and against which requirement. Each person sees the view for their role, and only what they are allowed to see. Try it: choose a role, then click a compartment.

How the role views work. This draws on early research from the MIT Media Lab (Hossein Rahnama, called Perspective-Aware AI): one 3D model, and each person sees the version their job needs, with access set by role. It has never been used in shipbuilding, so treat it as a direction to test at the Halifax yard, not a finished tool. The names and records shown here are made-up examples.

Everything in sync: the people, the plan, and the record

The build works when the right people reach competence on time, the overseers agree in one place, and the record keeps up. Here is who it takes, and how the pieces line up on the road to first delivery in the early 2030s.

The people who oversee it

The yard planner, the Navy sponsor, the government, the safety regulator, the Department of National Defence, and, on the submarine side, allied partners in Germany and Norway. Each sees the same ship filtered to their role: that is the decision room in view 4.

How it works in sync: the road to the early 2030s

Before the build

PRE-FLIGHT

Design and planning. VR pre-training brings welders and fitters up to competence before they touch a live hull, so the workforce is ready when each stage needs it. Stabilizing the design early is the single biggest lever against rework.

During the build

IN-FLIGHT

Assembly, hull join, and the long outfitting phase. The shared decision room gives every group one current model and one record, so out-of-sequence work and rework shrink, and design changes are agreed once, in one place, instead of through email chains.

Delivery

LANDING

Integration, trials, and handover. A clean, auditable record of who approved what, against which requirement, speeds sign-off at the hard gates. That helps the first ship land on its early-2030s target, and the pattern repeat across the fleet.

The honest gate stays. Only VR training is proven. The decision room is a promising direction built on early MIT research. AR is conditional. Nothing in this section changes that ranking.

Keep it simple, and start small

The analysis and the models run on their own computers, where they already live. The XR room only shows the results. No full automated digital copy of the ship: this problem does not need one, and building one would only add cost.

The close: build strong internally. Irving builds the hulls in Halifax, Seaspan builds in Vancouver, marine-technology firms like Kraken are growing in the region, and a governed decision room gives everyone one shared ship and one clear record, honest about what is proven and what is not. Getting these shared decisions right is part of how Canada meets its NATO, NORAD, and Arctic commitments on time, keeps public money accountable with a clear record, and builds one governed way of deciding that could carry across Irving, Seaspan, the submarine program, and Arctic surveillance. The prize is a repeatable, auditable way to decide, not a one-off tool.
