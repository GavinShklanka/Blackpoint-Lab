The problem

Roughly 7,000 people a year give up and walk out.

At Cape Breton Regional Hospital's emergency department, the province's published 2023-24 figures describe a hard pattern: about 38,900 visits a year, an average wait of 5.73 hours to see a physician, and 18.24 percent of patients leaving without ever being seen. That last figure is the crisis. It represents thousands of people who arrive needing care and leave without it.

The analysis set out to answer a question that is simple to ask and hard to resolve: who these patients are, why they leave, and whether either of two practical changes would fix it.

What the model represents

A working model of the department, calibrated to reality.

The model reproduces both published figures at once, the 5.73 hour wait and the 18.24 percent walkout rate. Calibration closed only when ten independent runs held both figures inside their confidence intervals, rather than relying on a single fortunate result.

The model fills the department the way the real one fills. Patients arrive on a realistic daily rhythm, a triage step sorts them by urgency, and physicians always see the sickest first. At this site about three in four arrivals are high-urgency, so less-urgent patients are repeatedly pushed to the back of the line. The walkouts are not programmed in. They emerge from that pressure on their own, and the model reproduces the real rate.

How the simulation works, one layer deeper

The engine is a discrete-event simulation built in AnyLogic. Patients arrive on a time-of-day curve calibrated so the year produces the real annual volume, and every run validates that count before any other result is trusted. Seeing a physician requires a physician and a staffed bed at the same moment. Admitted patients then board about 17.5 hours waiting for an inpatient bed and hold their emergency bed the whole time, which starves bed supply for everyone behind them. The model holds staffed beds at 22 of 36 physical, consistent with a reported 80 percent LPN vacancy. Staffing levels are stated assumptions where no public source exists, and every parameter carries an evidence tier. Both interventions live in one model as parameters, so the defaults reproduce the baseline exactly and each scenario changes only what it is meant to change.

Say
  • Discrete-event simulation, AnyLogic.
  • Calibrated to wait 5.73 h and LWBS 18.24 percent at the same time.
  • 10 replications, 95 percent confidence intervals.
  • 76 percent high-acuity (CTAS 1-3).
  • Seeing a physician needs a physician and a staffed bed together.

What the analysis tested

Two practical changes, alone and together.

Both target the same underserved group, the less-urgent patients who account for most of the walkouts. One change works before the front door, the other just inside it.

Scenario A

Community diversion

At triage, route one in five less-urgent patients to community care instead: a Nova Scotia urgent treatment centre, a pharmacist who can prescribe and assess, or provincial virtual care. Fewer low-urgency patients enter the waiting room at all.

Scenario B

Nurse-practitioner fast-track

Open a parallel fast-track lane staffed by a single nurse practitioner who sees only less-urgent patients, so they no longer compete with the sickest patients for a physician and a bed.

How each change works in the real department.

Scenario A, community rerouting. The model redirects a fifth of low-acuity (CTAS 4-5) arrivals at triage to capacity that already exists in the patient's own community: Nova Scotia urgent treatment centres, pharmacist prescribing and assessment, and provincial virtual care. These patients are tracked as redirected, not abandoned. The support condition is stated plainly: this only works if the receiving capacity is real and same-day, so the recommendation pairs the diversion fraction with a stated assumption about community appointment availability, labeled an assumption rather than a measured input. The model captures the routing effect only. The booking and coordination that would happen in the real world is named as out of scope for the simulation.

How the model handles it

Diversion is a single branch at triage. A set share of low-acuity patients is routed to community care; everyone else continues into the main physician queue exactly as in the baseline. The model adds no special handling for the patients who are not diverted: they wait under the same priority order and the same patience limit, which is why a share of them still leaves in Scenario A.

Scenario B, what the freed physician time buys. Moving low-acuity patients to the nurse-practitioner lane clears them out of the physician queue, which gives physicians time back to work the high-acuity queue. With CTAS 4-5 handled in parallel, physicians clear the CTAS 1-3 backlog faster, taking the sickest first and then working CTAS 3, the largest high-acuity tier and the one most at risk of deteriorating while waiting. The visible signature in the results is the physician queue dropping from about 41 waiting to about 17, even though physicians never see a single fast-track patient. This is the equity point: the priority discipline that protects the sickest is exactly what starves CTAS 4-5, so the fast-track lane is a structural correction for the group the system is built to deprioritize.

Effect modeled · ambulance lane is a future extension

What clearing low-acuity demand buys, in plain terms: pulling CTAS 4-5 patients out of the main queue, by diversion ahead of the door or a fast-track lane behind it, leaves the main emergency queue freer for the sickest patients and for ambulance (Emergency Health Services) arrivals. The model shows this effect indirectly: the physician queue falls from about 41 people waiting to about 17 once low-acuity demand is handled elsewhere, which is headroom the department can redirect to genuine emergencies. This study models that freed-capacity effect. A dedicated ambulance-arrival lane is named as a future extension, not a mechanism built into the current model.

Say
  • Scenario A: divert 20 percent of CTAS 4-5 at triage to existing community capacity.
  • Scenario B: one nurse-practitioner fast-track lane for low-acuity only.
  • Both target the same CTAS 4-5 population; tested alone and combined.
  • B signature: physician queue falls from about 41 to about 17 with no fast-track patient ever seen by a physician.

What the analysis found

One nurse practitioner does what diversion cannot.

1

The surprise: diversion lowers walkouts but cannot move the wait.

Diverting one in five low-urgency patients cuts walkouts from 19.0 to 15.7 percent, yet the average wait barely changes, from 5.51 to 5.43 hours. The reason: staffed beds run at about 98 percent occupancy in every scenario. The wait is a bed problem caused by admitted patients boarding for hours, and a low-urgency change cannot reach it.

2

The win: one fast-track nurse practitioner nearly ends the walkouts.

A single nurse practitioner in a fast-track lane collapses walkouts from 19.0 to 2.5 percent and cuts the average wait to a provider to 3.66 hours. Fast-track patients wait about 6 minutes, there were zero fast-track walkouts across ten simulated years, and the nurse practitioner is busy only about 35 percent of the time. One hire absorbs the entire underserved group.

3

The interaction: combining the two substitutes rather than adds.

Run together, the two changes do not stack, because both serve the same low-urgency patients. Inside the building the combined result matches the fast-track alone. What the combination adds is about 1,816 patients a year whose needs are met in their own community instead of in an emergency department.

Why walkouts compound

A patient who leaves without being seen does not stop being sick. Without reliable access to care, a condition that was low-acuity at the door can worsen and return later as a higher-acuity visit, often by ambulance, adding load back onto the same department. Reducing walkouts is not only a service-quality measure; it lowers the repeat and escalation demand the department faces later.

LWBS% = 3.18 - 0.46 · ln(nNPPA)   R² = 0.39

One nurse practitioner does almost all the work. Adding more barely moves the result and leaves them idle: utilization halves with each added provider (35.6%, 17.1%, 11.6%, 8.8%). The low R-squared is itself the point, beyond the first provider the points scatter within run-to-run noise.

How reality was encoded.

The model is only as trustworthy as the real-world detail inside it. Four things were built in deliberately, and the assumptions and blind spots are stated alongside them.

Figure 5 · Arrivals through the day

Realistic patient traffic. Arrivals ramp through the morning, peak in the late afternoon and early evening, and fall overnight. The year still totals the real annual volume, validated on every run before any result is trusted.

The parameters, in plain language

The model uses 22 staffed beds, not the 36 the building physically has, because the limit is how many beds the hospital can staff, not how many rooms exist. The number of physicians and nurses on shift. The average time a diagnostic test takes. How long admitted patients wait for an inpatient bed. And how a patient's patience changes with urgency.

Assumptions, stated honestly

Physician and nurse counts are assumptions where no public source reports an annual average. Diagnostic time is a held estimate. Community capacity for diversion is an assumption about same-day availability. Each is labeled an assumption, not a measured input.

Limitations

The model releases patients from the physician queue, so anyone who left before triage in the real department is invisible to it. The site's acuity mix is more acute than the provincial average, so results are specific to Cape Breton. And the coordination layer that would book community appointments is not simulated.

Say
  • A: walkouts 19.0 to 15.7 percent, wait unchanged at 5.5 to 5.4 h.
  • B: walkouts to 2.5 percent, wait to 3.7 h, NP busy about 35 percent.
  • Beds at about 98 percent in every scenario, the binding constraint.
  • A+B substitutes, adds about 1,816 community resolutions a year.
  • Diversion fit linear (R-squared 0.90); fast-track returns diminish after one NP.

Glossary

What each term means in this model.

CTAS (Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale)
The 1-to-5 urgency rating assigned at triage. In this model it sets queue priority and patience: CTAS 1-3 are high-acuity and seen first; CTAS 4-5 are low-acuity and the population the two interventions target.
LWBS (Left Without Being Seen)
A patient who leaves before any provider sees them. In this model it is not programmed; it emerges when a low-acuity patient's patience runs out while higher-acuity patients keep taking priority.
TIS (Time In System)
Total time from arrival to leaving, reported separately for discharged and admitted patients. Admitted TIS stays near 28 hours in every scenario because of bed boarding.
WIP (Work In Progress)
The number of patients physically in the department at once.
Qavg / Qmax
The average and peak number of patients waiting in the physician queue.
NP / PA (Nurse Practitioner / Physician Assistant)
The fast-track provider in Scenario B, serving only low-acuity patients in a parallel lane.
divertFrac
The share of low-acuity patients routed to community care at triage; the continuous knob swept in the dose-response chart.
nNPPA
The number of nurse practitioners staffing the fast-track lane.
Throughput
Patients fully handled per hour. The blended total counts both the main lane and the fast-track lane; the dashboard's main-lane figure alone understates the system when the fast lane is active.
Boarding
Admitted patients holding an ED bed while waiting for an inpatient bed, about 17.5 hours here; the root cause of the wait that no low-acuity intervention can move.

The recommendation, honestly stated

Adopt both, and be clear about why.

The recommendation is to adopt the combination: community diversion plus a one-nurse-practitioner fast-track. It gives the same underserved population two independent paths to care, one before the front door and one behind it.

The analysis states plainly what the numbers say. Inside the department, the combination performs the same as the fast-track alone, so the second change earns its place at the system level, by resolving roughly 1,800 patients a year in their own community rather than by speeding the emergency department further.

It also names what nothing fixed. Admitted patients still spend about 28 hours in the system because inpatient beds are scarce. Boarding is the real constraint on waits, and it is the clear next investigation.

Say
  • Recommend A+B.
  • In-department performance equals B alone, stated openly.
  • Extra value is about 1,816 patients a year resolved in community.
  • Boarding at about 28 h admitted time-in-system is untouched, the next frontier.

Built as an MBAN 5630 graduate project at Saint Mary's University and calibrated to the Nova Scotia ED Accountability Report 2023-24. Staffing levels are labeled assumptions where no public source reports them. Staffed beds are set at 22 of 36 physical, evidenced by a reported 80 percent LPN vacancy and confirmed by a sensitivity run, at 36 staffed beds the crisis disappears entirely. One replication was excluded by a pre-registered validation rule when its annual volume fell outside the acceptance band, and was replaced; every such event is dated in the project's failure-mode register. This is a decision-support model for capacity planning, not an operational tool.
