The problem

Roughly 7,000 people a year give up and walk out.

At Cape Breton Regional Hospital's emergency department, the province's published 2023-24 figures tell a hard story: about 38,900 visits a year, an average wait of 5.73 hours to see a physician, and 18.24 percent of patients leaving without ever being seen. That last number is the crisis. It is thousands of people who arrive needing care and leave without it.

The question we set out to answer was simple to ask and hard to answer: who are these patients, why do they leave, and would either of two practical changes actually fix it.

What we built

A working model of the department, calibrated to reality.

We built a simulation of the emergency department and tuned it until it reproduced both published figures at once, the 5.73 hour wait and the 18.24 percent walkout rate, then checked it across ten independent runs with confidence intervals rather than trusting a single lucky result.

The model fills the department the way the real one fills: patients arrive on a realistic daily rhythm, a triage step sorts them by urgency, and physicians always see the sickest first. At this site about three in four arrivals are high-urgency, so the less-urgent patients keep getting pushed to the back of the line. The walkouts emerge from that pressure on their own. We did not program a walkout rate into the model; the model produced the real one.

How the simulation works, one layer deeper

The engine is a discrete-event simulation built in AnyLogic. Patients arrive on a time-of-day curve calibrated so the year produces the real annual volume, and every run validates that count before any other result is trusted. Seeing a physician requires a physician and a staffed bed at the same moment. Admitted patients then board about 17.5 hours waiting for an inpatient bed and hold their emergency bed the whole time, which is what starves bed supply for everyone behind them. Staffed beds are set at 22 of 36 physical, consistent with a reported 80 percent LPN vacancy. Staffing levels are stated assumptions where no public source exists, and every parameter carries an evidence tier. Both interventions live in one model as parameters, so the defaults reproduce the baseline exactly and each scenario changes only what it is meant to change.

What we tested

Two practical changes, alone and together.

Both target the same underserved group, the less-urgent patients who do most of the walking out. One change works before the front door, the other just inside it.

Scenario A

Community diversion

At triage, route one in five less-urgent patients to community care instead: an urgent treatment centre, a pharmacist who can prescribe, or a virtual visit. Fewer low-urgency patients enter the waiting room at all.

Scenario B

Nurse-practitioner fast-track

Open a parallel fast-track lane staffed by a single nurse practitioner who sees only less-urgent patients, so they no longer compete with the sickest patients for a physician and a bed.

What we found

One nurse practitioner does what diversion cannot.

1

The surprise: diversion lowers walkouts but cannot move the wait.

Diverting one in five low-urgency patients cuts walkouts from 19.0 to 15.7 percent, yet the average wait barely changes, from 5.51 to 5.43 hours. The reason: staffed beds run at 98 percent occupancy in every scenario. The wait is a bed problem caused by admitted patients boarding for hours, and a low-urgency change cannot reach it.

2

The win: one fast-track nurse practitioner nearly ends the walkouts.

A single nurse practitioner in a fast-track lane collapses walkouts from 19.0 to 2.5 percent and cuts the average wait to a provider to 3.66 hours. Fast-track patients wait about 6 minutes, there were zero fast-track walkouts across ten simulated years, and the nurse practitioner is busy only 36 percent of the time. One hire absorbs the entire underserved group.

3

The interaction: combining the two substitutes rather than adds.

Run together, the two changes do not stack, because both serve the same low-urgency patients. Inside the building the combined result matches the fast-track alone. What the combination adds is about 1,816 patients a year whose needs are met in their own community instead of in an emergency department.

The recommendation, honestly stated

Adopt both, and be clear about why.

We recommend the combination: community diversion plus a one-nurse-practitioner fast-track. It gives the same underserved population two independent paths to care, one before the front door and one behind it.

We say plainly what the numbers say. Inside the department, the combination performs the same as the fast-track alone, so the second change earns its place at the system level, by resolving roughly 1,800 patients a year in their own community rather than by speeding the emergency department further.

And we name what nothing fixed. Admitted patients still spend about 28 hours in the system because inpatient beds are scarce. Boarding is the real constraint on waits, and it is the clear next investigation.

On the record

Built as an MBAN 5630 graduate project at Saint Mary's University and calibrated to the Nova Scotia ED Accountability Report 2023-24. Staffing levels are labeled assumptions where no public source reports them. Staffed beds are set at 22 of 36 physical, evidenced by a reported 80 percent LPN vacancy and confirmed by a sensitivity run, at 36 staffed beds the crisis disappears entirely. One replication was excluded by a pre-registered validation rule when its annual volume fell outside the acceptance band, and was replaced; every such event is dated in the project's failure-mode register. This is a decision-support model for capacity planning, not an operational tool.
