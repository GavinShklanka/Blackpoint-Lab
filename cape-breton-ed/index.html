The problem

Roughly 7,000 people a year give up and walk out.

At Cape Breton Regional Hospital's emergency department, the province's published 2023-24 figures describe a hard pattern: about 38,900 visits a year, an average wait of 5.73 hours to see a physician, and 18.24 percent of patients leaving without ever being seen. That last figure is the crisis. It represents thousands of people who arrive needing care and leave without it.

The analysis set out to answer a question that is simple to ask and hard to resolve: who these patients are, why they leave, and whether either of two practical changes would fix it.

  • 38,900 visits per year.

What the model represents

A working model of the department, calibrated to reality.

The model reproduces both published figures at once, the 5.73 hour wait and the 18.24 percent walkout rate. Calibration closed only when ten independent runs held both figures inside their confidence intervals, rather than relying on a single fortunate result.

The model fills the department the way the real one fills. Patients arrive on a realistic daily rhythm, a triage step sorts them by urgency, and physicians always see the sickest first. At this site about three in four arrivals are high-urgency, so less-urgent patients are repeatedly pushed to the back of the line. The walkouts are not programmed in. They emerge from that pressure on their own, and the model reproduces the real rate.

How the simulation works, one layer deeper

The engine is a discrete-event simulation built in AnyLogic. Patients arrive on a time-of-day curve calibrated so the year produces the real annual volume, and every run validates that count before any other result is trusted. Seeing a physician requires a physician and a staffed bed at the same moment. Admitted patients then board about 17.5 hours waiting for an inpatient bed and hold their emergency bed the whole time, which starves bed supply for everyone behind them. The model holds staffed beds at 22 of 36 physical, consistent with a reported 80 percent LPN vacancy. Staffing levels are stated assumptions where no public source exists, and every parameter carries an evidence tier. Both interventions live in one model as parameters, so the defaults reproduce the baseline exactly and each scenario changes only what it is meant to change.

What the analysis tested

Two practical changes, alone and together.

Both target the same underserved group, the less-urgent patients who account for most of the walkouts. One change works before the front door, the other just inside it.

Scenario A

Community diversion

At triage, route one in five less-urgent patients to community care instead: a Nova Scotia urgent treatment centre, a pharmacist who can prescribe and assess, or provincial virtual care. Fewer low-urgency patients enter the waiting room at all.

Scenario B

Nurse-practitioner fast-track

Open a parallel fast-track lane staffed by a single nurse practitioner who sees only less-urgent patients, so they no longer compete with the sickest patients for a physician and a bed.

How each change works in the real department.

Scenario A, community rerouting. The model redirects a fifth of low-acuity (CTAS 4-5) arrivals at triage to capacity that already exists in the patient's own community: Nova Scotia urgent treatment centres, pharmacist prescribing and assessment, and provincial virtual care. These patients are tracked as redirected, not abandoned. The support condition is stated plainly: this only works if the receiving capacity is real and same-day, so the recommendation pairs the diversion fraction with a stated assumption about community appointment availability, labeled an assumption rather than a measured input. The model captures the routing effect only. The booking and coordination that would happen in the real world is named as out of scope for the simulation.

How the model handles it

Diversion is a single branch at triage. A set share of low-acuity patients is routed to community care; everyone else continues into the main physician queue exactly as in the baseline. The model adds no special handling for the patients who are not diverted: they wait under the same priority order and the same patience limit, which is why a share of them still leaves in Scenario A.

Scenario B, what the freed physician time buys. Moving low-acuity patients to the nurse-practitioner lane clears them out of the physician queue, which gives physicians time back to work the high-acuity queue. With CTAS 4-5 handled in parallel, physicians clear the CTAS 1-3 backlog faster, taking the sickest first and then working CTAS 3, the largest high-acuity tier and the one most at risk of deteriorating while waiting. The visible signature in the results is the physician queue dropping from about 41 waiting to about 17, even though physicians never see a single fast-track patient. This is the equity point: the priority discipline that protects the sickest is exactly what starves CTAS 4-5, so the fast-track lane is a structural correction for the group the system is built to deprioritize.

Effect modeled · ambulance lane is a future extension

What clearing low-acuity demand buys, in plain terms: pulling CTAS 4-5 patients out of the main queue, by diversion ahead of the door or a fast-track lane behind it, leaves the main emergency queue freer for the sickest patients and for ambulance (Emergency Health Services) arrivals. The model shows this effect indirectly: the physician queue falls from about 41 people waiting to about 17 once low-acuity demand is handled elsewhere, which is headroom the department can redirect to genuine emergencies. This study models that freed-capacity effect. A dedicated ambulance-arrival lane is named as a future extension, not a mechanism built into the current model.

Say
  • Scenario A: divert 20 percent of CTAS 4-5 at triage to existing community capacity.
  • Scenario B: one nurse-practitioner fast-track lane for low-acuity only.
  • Both target the same CTAS 4-5 population; tested alone and combined.
  • B signature: physician queue falls from about 41 to about 17 with no fast-track patient ever seen by a physician.

What the analysis found

One nurse practitioner does what diversion cannot.

1

The surprise: diversion lowers walkouts but cannot move the wait.

Diverting one in five low-urgency patients cuts walkouts from 19.0 to 15.7 percent, yet the average wait barely changes, from 5.51 to 5.43 hours. The reason: staffed beds run at about 98 percent occupancy in every scenario. The wait is a bed problem caused by admitted patients boarding for hours, and a low-urgency change cannot reach it.

2

The win: one fast-track nurse practitioner nearly ends the walkouts.

A single nurse practitioner in a fast-track lane collapses walkouts from 19.0 to 2.5 percent and cuts the average wait to a provider to 3.66 hours. Fast-track patients wait about 6 minutes, there were zero fast-track walkouts across ten simulated years, and the nurse practitioner is busy only about 35 percent of the time. One hire absorbs the entire underserved group.

3

The interaction: combining the two substitutes rather than adds.

Run together, the two changes do not stack, because both serve the same low-urgency patients. Inside the building the combined result matches the fast-track alone. What the combination adds is about 1,816 patients a year whose needs are met in their own community instead of in an emergency department.

Why walkouts compound

A patient who leaves without being seen does not stop being sick. Without reliable access to care, a condition that was low-acuity at the door can worsen and return later as a higher-acuity visit, often by ambulance, adding load back onto the same department. Reducing walkouts is not only a service-quality measure; it lowers the repeat and escalation demand the department faces later.

How reality was encoded.

The model is only as trustworthy as the real-world detail inside it. Four things were built in deliberately, and the assumptions and blind spots are stated alongside them.

The parameters, in plain language

The model uses 22 staffed beds, not the 36 the building physically has, because the limit is how many beds the hospital can staff, not how many rooms exist. The number of physicians and nurses on shift. The average time a diagnostic test takes. How long admitted patients wait for an inpatient bed. And how a patient's patience changes with urgency.

Assumptions, stated honestly

Physician and nurse counts are assumptions where no public source reports an annual average. Diagnostic time is a held estimate. Community capacity for diversion is an assumption about same-day availability. Each is labeled an assumption, not a measured input.

Limitations

The model releases patients from the physician queue, so anyone who left before triage in the real department is invisible to it. The site's acuity mix is more acute than the provincial average, so results are specific to Cape Breton. And the coordination layer that would book community appointments is not simulated.

  • B: walkouts to 2.5 percent, wait to 3.7 h, NP busy about 35 percent.
  • Beds at about 98 percent in every scenario, the binding constraint.
  • A+B substitutes, adds about 1,816 community resolutions a year.
  • Diversion fit linear (R-squared 0.90); fast-track returns diminish after one NP.

Glossary

What each term means in this model.

CTAS (Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale)
The 1-to-5 urgency rating assigned at triage. In this model it sets queue priority and patience: CTAS 1-3 are high-acuity and seen first; CTAS 4-5 are low-acuity and the population the two interventions target.
LWBS (Left Without Being Seen)
A patient who leaves before any provider sees them. In this model it is not programmed; it emerges when a low-acuity patient's patience runs out while higher-acuity patients keep taking priority.
TIS (Time In System)
Total time from arrival to leaving, reported separately for discharged and admitted patients. Admitted TIS stays near 28 hours in every scenario because of bed boarding.
WIP (Work In Progress)
The number of patients physically in the department at once.
Qavg / Qmax
The average and peak number of patients waiting in the physician queue.
NP / PA (Nurse Practitioner / Physician Assistant)
The fast-track provider in Scenario B, serving only low-acuity patients in a parallel lane.
divertFrac
The share of low-acuity patients routed to community care at triage; the continuous knob swept in the dose-response chart.
nNPPA
The number of nurse practitioners staffing the fast-track lane.
Throughput
Patients fully handled per hour. The blended total counts both the main lane and the fast-track lane; the dashboard's main-lane figure alone understates the system when the fast lane is active.
Boarding
Admitted patients holding an ED bed while waiting for an inpatient bed, about 17.5 hours here; the root cause of the wait that no low-acuity intervention can move.

The recommendation, honestly stated

Adopt both, and be clear about why.

The recommendation is to adopt the combination: community diversion plus a one-nurse-practitioner fast-track. It gives the same underserved population two independent paths to care, one before the front door and one behind it.

The analysis states plainly what the numbers say. Inside the department, the combination performs the same as the fast-track alone, so the second change earns its place at the system level, by resolving roughly 1,800 patients a year in their own community rather than by speeding the emergency department further.

It also names what nothing fixed. Admitted patients still spend about 28 hours in the system because inpatient beds are scarce. Boarding is the real constraint on waits, and it is the clear next investigation.

Say
  • Recommend A+B.
  • In-department performance equals B alone, stated openly.
  • Extra value is about 1,816 patients a year resolved in community.
  • Boarding at about 28 h admitted time-in-system is untouched, the next frontier.

Project 2 · 2026 · n = 20

The back door: modeling the whole system.

Project 1 mapped the front door of the emergency department, the people waiting to be seen and the one in five who left without care, and found two fixes that cut walkouts: a fast-track lane and community diversion. But it ended on an open question. Admitted patients were still spending more than a day stranded in the emergency department, waiting for a bed on a hospital ward upstairs, and the Project 1 model could not see why, because it stopped at the emergency-department doors. Project 2 rebuilt the model to follow patients through the back door: into the ward, through the long wait for a long-term-care placement, and back out to the community. Because the new model captures the whole system, its starting numbers differ slightly from Project 1's front-door-only figures. Everything below is the Project 2 whole-system model, twenty runs per scenario.

The target

Both projects share one goal: a hospital full enough to run efficiently, but not so full it fails patients. In practice that means three things at once, a ward kept below the safety line, walkouts driven down, and waits brought under target. Project 1 found the levers for the front door. Project 2 found the levers for the back door. The scorecard for how close the full package gets is further down.

Four changes were tested. The department turns out to have two separate jams that need two separate fixes.

The two-door finding.

The back door is the admitted-patient wait for a ward bed, called boarding. Only one thing moves it: adding ward beds. Seventy-two more beds cut that wait from about 30 hours to 28 and emptied the ward from 91 percent full to 61.

The front door is the waiting room, where people sit and some leave. Two things move it: a fast-track lane staffed by a nurse practitioner, and sending lower-urgency patients to community services. The fast-track lane alone cut walkouts from about 23 percent to under 5. Run all four changes together and walkouts fall to under 3 percent, the admitted wait holds at 28 hours, and the ward sits at 61 percent.

The reversal, stated honestly

Adding beds was expected to fail, on the theory that long-stay patients waiting for nursing-home placement would quietly refill them. At Cape Breton's placement levels, that did not happen. The new beds worked and stayed largely empty. Freeing the long-stay beds is a separate lever: faster nursing-home placement, which recovered about eight blocked beds without changing the emergency-department numbers at all. This is realistic because Cape Breton's typical placement wait is about 115 days against a provincial average near 75; closing that gap is the lever, and in the model it freed about eight ward beds and dropped the long-stay share from about 17 percent to 13.

How the model works

What we built, in plain language.

Project 2 follows a patient past the emergency-department doors and into the hospital. Five pieces of the model carry that story.

The admission decision (selectAdmit)
When treatment ends, most patients go home, but some are sick enough to need a hospital bed upstairs. This is that fork, the moment a visit becomes an admission. About one in nine arrivals takes it.
The emergency-department beds (bedPool)
The 22 staffed treatment beds. A patient holds one until they leave or move upstairs. When all 22 are full, the next patient waits. These stay near full in every scenario, which is the punchline at the end.
The ward beds upstairs (wardPool)
The 162 inpatient beds. An admitted patient needs one to leave the emergency department. When the ward is full, that patient is stranded in an ED bed. That wait is the back-door jam, and it is why an admitted patient spends about 30 hours here before reaching the ward.
The placement pipeline (dischargeDestinationPool)
The route to a long-term-care or home-care spot for patients ready to leave the hospital. We kept the spots plentiful on purpose, because the real Cape Breton bottleneck is the wait to get one, not the number of spots.
The long-stay wait (ALC placement)
A patient who is medically done but waiting for placement keeps holding a ward bed until one is found. We model that wait on Cape Breton's real numbers: about 115 days, ranging from under two months to over six.

How a patient moves through the model

One pass through the system, step by step.

  1. Arrival and triage (source, triage). A patient arrives on the real daily rhythm and is sorted by urgency on the five-level triage scale.
  2. The diversion fork (selectDivert, selectCommunity). A share of the lowest-urgency patients is routed out to community services and split across five channels.
  3. Fast-track or physician (selectFastTrack to the nurse-practitioner lane: ftQueue, ftSeize, ftService, ftRelease; or the main line: physicianQueue, seizeMD, physicianDelay, releaseMD). Quick cases clear through the NP lane; the rest wait for a physician and a bed.
  4. Diagnostics (diagnosticDelay). Tests and results add time before a disposition is decided.
  5. The admission fork (selectAdmit). Most patients are discharged (releaseBedDisc, tisEndDisc, dischargeSink). Some are admitted and continue.
  6. Boarding (boardDelay, seizeWardBg_ED, releaseBedAdmit). The admitted patient waits for a ward bed while still holding an ED bed. This is the back-door jam, and the clock for admitted time in the ED stops here (tisEndAdmit).
  7. The ward stay (acuteHold). The patient occupies a ward bed for the clinical length of stay.
  8. The long-stay fork (selectALC). Most patients finish and leave (releaseWardBg_ED, admitSink). The long-stay few seize a placement slot and wait (seizeDischargeDest, alcPlacementHold for the 115-day placement wait, releaseDischargeDest, releaseWardBgALC), holding the ward bed the whole time.
  9. The background ward census (wardDemandSource, seizeWardBg, wardHoldBg, releaseWardBg). A steady stream of inpatient admissions from the rest of the system keeps the ward realistically full, so the ED competes for real beds.
  10. Measurement (tisStart at arrival, tisEndAdmit and tisEndDisc at exit). Time in the system is measured at the ED exit, not after the ward stay, so the numbers describe the emergency-department experience.

The community split

Where the diverted patients go.

Of the lower-urgency patients redirected from the emergency department, the model sends about a third to an urgent treatment centre, just over a third to community pharmacies for minor ailments, one in seven to virtual care, one in eight to a collaborative care clinic, and a small group, about five percent, to a referral path.

On the referral channel

That last group, about five percent, are the people who would hit a closed or fully booked service and risk coming back to the emergency department. The model counts how many are at that risk. It does not estimate whether they return, because it does not produce a return or readmission rate.

What each channel is, and why it holds.

Patients are sorted by the Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale. Levels 4 and 5, the less-urgent and non-urgent cases, are what community services are designed to treat, which is what makes the diversion clinically sound rather than a shortcut. Each channel is an existing Nova Scotia service, and the split follows the kinds of complaints each one is built for.

Urgent treatment centre, 33 percent
Walk-in care for injuries and acute-but-minor problems: musculoskeletal injuries, lacerations, mild mental-health needs. Nova Scotia urgent treatment centres recorded about 60,611 visits in 2023-24, so the capacity to absorb this load is real, not hypothetical.
Community pharmacy, 36 percent
The largest channel, because pharmacists now have a funded scope for common conditions: skin complaints, urinary infections, eye issues, mild respiratory problems, and prescription renewals. These are high-volume, low-acuity visits that do not need an emergency department.
Virtual care, 14 percent
VirtualCareNS connects patients to a provider remotely, for low-acuity advice and the overflow a pharmacist could also handle.
Collaborative care clinic, 12 percent
Team-based primary care, a family physician working with nurses and other providers, for non-specific abdominal pain and other lower-acuity complaints that need an assessment but not emergency resources. It is the channel for patients who really need a primary-care visit they could not get, which is often why they came to the emergency department in the first place.
Referral, at risk, 5 percent
The honest channel. These are patients who would reach a closed or fully booked service and could bounce back to the emergency department. The model counts how many are at that risk; it does not claim whether they return, because it does not produce a return rate.

The split itself is a Tier C assumption derived from the community resource docket's complaint categories and is swept, because the exact Cape Breton triage-4-and-5 complaint mix is not published.

What it means for one patient

One person, four changes.

Picture a 70-year-old who arrives on a busy Tuesday evening with a minor but worrying complaint. In today's department she waits nearly six hours, and one in five people like her give up and leave before anyone sees them.

Now turn on the four changes. If her complaint is low-urgency, she never sits in that waiting room; she is handled the same day at an urgent treatment centre or a pharmacy nearby. If she does need the emergency department, a nurse practitioner in the fast-track lane sees her in a fraction of the time. If she turns out to be sick enough to admit, a ward bed is ready upstairs instead of a 30-hour hold in a hallway. And the patient who held that bed last week, medically ready but waiting for a nursing-home place, was moved on weeks sooner, which is why the bed was free for her.

No single change does all of this. Together they make the whole path flow, and they ease not just the emergency department but the community and long-term-care system wrapped around it.

The honest tail

Averages hide the worst cases.

Even with every change in place, the worst-served tenth of patients still wait about 13.5 hours to see a provider, and the longest boarding waits stay near two days.

The fast-track lane is the only change that shortens the very longest waits to be seen. Extra beds are the only thing that shortens the very longest boarding waits.

The honest residual

Even with everything switched on, the emergency department's own 22 treatment beds stay about 97 percent full, above the 85 percent level considered safe. The next thing to fix, after this package, is emergency-department bed and staff capacity itself. This study does not solve that.

How the build maps to the numbers

Every metric is measured off a real part of the model.

A number is only trustworthy if you can point to where it comes from. Each headline metric is read directly from a block or a resource pool on the canvas, not computed on the side.

Door-to-doctor wait
The clock from triage (tisStart) to the moment a provider is seized (the physician or fast-track service). It measures the waiting room, nothing after.
Walkouts (LWBS)
The count at lwbsSink over arrivals. A patient leaves when the wait exceeds their patience, set by triage level (patienceByCtas), so the walkout rate is an emergent result, not an input.
Admitted time in ED
tisStart to tisEndAdmit, which sits before the ward stay (acuteHold). The metric describes the emergency-department experience, including boarding, and deliberately stops at the ED exit.
Ward occupancy and the acute-versus-ALC split
Read from wardPool utilization() as a year-long time average, separated by the long-stay flag, so it is a true bed-day share rather than an end-of-run snapshot.
ALC bed-day share
Long-stay beds over total occupied ward beds, the same definition national reporting uses, which is why it can be compared to the 17 to 28 percent band.
ED bed utilization
bedPool utilization(), the share of the 22 staffed beds in use.
Throughput
Every patient who completes care, counted at dischargeSink, admitSink, and ftSink, divided by time. The fast-track term is zero when the lane is off, so the same measure is correct in every scenario.
Why it is reproducible

Every scenario runs twenty times with a fixed per-replication seed, so the same scenario gives the same result and the differences between scenarios are tested with confidence intervals, not eyeballed. The baseline reproduces identically across the two builds, which is the check that the model was not quietly changed between runs.

Project 2 recommendation

Fund the package as coordinated moves.

To shorten the admitted-patient wait, add ward beds. To cut walkouts and waiting-room time, run the fast-track lane and community diversion together. To recover beds tied up by patients awaiting placement, speed up nursing-home placement.

Together they clear the ward-crowding and walkout problems and leave emergency-department bed capacity as the next target.

Honesty note on placement. The nursing-home placement rate used here is a Cape Breton-informed assumption tested across a range, not a published CBRH figure. All Project 2 values are means of twenty runs with 95% confidence intervals.
